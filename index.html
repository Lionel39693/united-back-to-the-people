A club built through generations

The story behind the badge.

From railway workers in Newton Heath to European champions, from heartbreak and relegation to rebuilding. This is a living history—and every generation has helped shape it.

1878 · The beginning

Newton Heath LYR is born

Manchester United began as Newton Heath Lancashire and Yorkshire Railway (LYR) Football Club, formed by railway workers who played against other departments and railway companies. The club's roots were local, working-class and built around community.

1902 · A new identity

Newton Heath becomes Manchester United

Financial trouble threatened the club's survival. Investment from local businessman John Henry Davies helped secure its future, and the name Manchester United was adopted in 1902.

1914–1918 · The First World War

Football gives way to war

The First World War brought League football to a halt after the 1914–15 season. United played in regional wartime competitions while many footballers joined the armed forces. Former United player Sandy Turnbull, a member of the 1909 FA Cup-winning side, was killed in action in May 1917. League football resumed in 1919, but the club and the country had been changed by the conflict.

1908–1910 · First foundations

First league title and a new home

United won their first league championship in 1908, followed by the FA Cup in 1909. In 1910, the club moved to Old Trafford, which became its home.

1939–1945 · The Second World War

War reaches Old Trafford

The outbreak of the Second World War stopped normal League football. Old Trafford was used by the military as a depot and suffered bombing raids in December 1940 and March 1941. The March raid caused extensive destruction, including damage to the main stand, offices and dressing rooms. United played home matches at Maine Road, Manchester City's ground, while Old Trafford was rebuilt. The stadium reopened in August 1949.

1945–1957 · The Busby Babes

Matt Busby builds a new United

After the Second World War, Sir Matt Busby and his staff built a team that blended young home-grown talent with experienced players. The Busby Babes won league titles in 1952, 1956 and 1957, capturing the imagination of supporters.

6 February 1958 · Munich

A day United will never forget

The Munich Air Disaster claimed the lives of 23 people, including eight Manchester United players. The club, families and football community were devastated. We remember every player, staff member, journalist, crew member and supporter affected. Their memory remains part of United's identity.

1963–1968 · Rebuilding and glory

From loss to the summit of Europe

Busby rebuilt the team. United won the FA Cup in 1963, league titles in 1965 and 1967, then became the first English club to win the European Cup in 1968, beating Benfica at Wembley.

1964 · 1966 · 1968 · Ballon d'Or history

United's three early Ballon d'Or winners

Denis Law won the 1964 Ballon d'Or, Bobby Charlton won in 1966, and George Best completed the sequence in 1968. Best's award came in the same year United conquered Europe.

1974–1975 · A painful fall and a quick return

Relegation—and promotion at the first attempt

United were relegated from the First Division at the end of the 1973–74 season, six years after winning the European Cup. Tommy Docherty's side responded by winning the Second Division title in 1974–75 and returning to the top flight. 1978 was not the relegation year; United finished 10th in the First Division in 1977–78.

November 1986 · A new manager

Alex Ferguson arrives

After winning major honours with Aberdeen, Alex Ferguson was appointed Manchester United manager in November 1986. He inherited a club that needed rebuilding and would require patience before the next great era arrived.

1988–1990 · The pressure years

Ferguson's toughest early test

United finished 11th in 1988–89, then 13th in 1989–90—Ferguson's lowest league finish as United manager. Calls for his dismissal grew, but the 1990 FA Cup triumph helped keep the rebuilding project alive. The 1989–90 season, rather than 1988–89, was the lowest league finish of his United tenure.

Newton Heath to the present day

The United Hall of Fame.

A growing record of the players who built this club, carried it through difficult eras, delivered unforgettable moments and represent its next generation. This is a curated tribute—not a claim that every supporter will agree on every name.

How to read the numbers: senior men's first-team competitive appearances and goals are shown where a consistent historical record is available. Assist tracking is incomplete for older eras and differs between databases, so we will only publish assist totals when the source and scope are clear. Goalkeeper clean sheets are listed separately; clean sheets are not an individual outfield-player statistic. Figures below are historical snapshots, not live counters.
Our standard for greatness: every position is judged on what the player contributed to United. Goals and assists matter, but so do defending, positioning, leadership, consistency, distribution, game control, saves, organisation, tactical intelligence and performances in decisive matches. A full-back does not need striker numbers; a centre-back does not need highlight-reel skills; a goalkeeper is not measured by goals. Quiet excellence counts.

The teams that made United.

A record of the great Manchester United sides, the campaigns that brought silverware home, and the academy pathway that has connected generations. The player lists below identify key contributors and are not presented as exhaustive registration lists for every competition.

The greatest United squads

1907/08 · First league title

Mangnall's first champions

Manager: Ernest Mangnall · Captain: Charlie Roberts

United's first league-winning side, built around the leadership of Roberts and the attacking quality of Billy Meredith, Sandy Turnbull and George Wall. This team established United as a major force.

1947/48 · FA Cup

Busby's first Wembley winners

Manager: Matt Busby · Captain: Johnny Carey

Beat Blackpool 4–2 in a famous final. Key names included Carey, Jack Rowley, Stan Pearson, Charlie Mitten, John Aston and Allenby Chilton. Six starters had come through United's youth ranks.

1955/56 and 1956/57 · League champions

The Busby Babes

Manager: Matt Busby · Coach: Jimmy Murphy

A fearless young side built around Duncan Edwards, Bobby Charlton, Tommy Taylor, Roger Byrne, Eddie Colman, David Pegg, Dennis Viollet, Mark Jones and Jackie Blanchflower. They won consecutive league titles before Munich changed the club forever.

1967/68 · European Cup

Busby's European champions

Manager: Matt Busby

The rebuilt side became the first English club to win the European Cup. The core included Bobby Charlton, George Best, Denis Law, Brian Kidd, Nobby Stiles, Pat Crerand, Bill Foulkes, David Sadler, John Aston and Alex Stepney. Charlton (twice), Best and Kidd scored in the 4–1 final win over Benfica.

1998/99 · The Treble

The Treble winners

Manager: Sir Alex Ferguson · Captain: Roy Keane

Peter Schmeichel, Gary Neville, Jaap Stam, Ronny Johnsen, Denis Irwin, David Beckham, Keane, Paul Scholes, Ryan Giggs, Dwight Yorke, Andy Cole, Teddy Sheringham, Ole Gunnar Solskjær, Nicky Butt and Jesper Blomqvist formed a squad that won the Premier League, FA Cup and Champions League.

2007/08 · Premier League and Champions League

The Moscow champions

Manager: Sir Alex Ferguson · Captain: Gary Neville / Rio Ferdinand on the pitch

Edwin van der Sar, Rio Ferdinand, Nemanja Vidić, Patrice Evra, Wes Brown, Michael Carrick, Paul Scholes, Owen Hargreaves, Cristiano Ronaldo, Wayne Rooney, Carlos Tevez, Ryan Giggs, Park Ji-sung, Nani and Anderson powered a brilliant team to the double.

2012/13 · Premier League

Ferguson's final champions

Manager: Sir Alex Ferguson

Robin van Persie's goals led the charge, supported by Wayne Rooney, Michael Carrick, Rio Ferdinand, Nemanja Vidić, Patrice Evra, David de Gea, Rafael, Javier Hernández, Danny Welbeck, Shinji Kagawa, Ryan Giggs and Paul Scholes. United secured Ferguson's 13th Premier League title.

2016/17 · League Cup and Europa League

Mourinho's cup winners

Manager: José Mourinho

David de Gea, Sergio Romero, Antonio Valencia, Eric Bailly, Chris Smalling, Daley Blind, Ander Herrera, Paul Pogba, Juan Mata, Henrikh Mkhitaryan, Marcus Rashford, Zlatan Ibrahimović, Marouane Fellaini and Jesse Lingard helped United win two major trophies.

2022/23 · League Cup

The Wembley return

Manager: Erik ten Hag · Captain: Bruno Fernandes

David de Gea, Lisandro Martínez, Raphaël Varane, Casemiro, Christian Eriksen, Bruno Fernandes, Marcus Rashford, Luke Shaw, Fred, Antony, Alejandro Garnacho, Aaron Wan-Bissaka and Wout Weghorst were among the contributors to United's first trophy in six years.

The Academy: a red thread through generations

United's academy is not a side story. It is part of the club's identity. From the Busby Babes to Beckham, McTominay and Mainoo, homegrown players have shaped the first team and its greatest nights.

1930s–1940s · Foundations

A pathway is built

United created an 'A' team in 1932 and launched its junior athletic club in 1938. Johnny Carey, Stan Pearson, John Aston and Charlie Mitten were among the early talents developed through the system.

1950s · Busby Babes

Youth becomes the first team

Matt Busby and Jimmy Murphy built around young players. Duncan Edwards, Bobby Charlton, Eddie Colman, David Pegg, Dennis Viollet and their teammates became a generation that won league titles before the Munich disaster.

1960s · Rebuilding

Homegrown European champions

George Best, Nobby Stiles, David Sadler and John Aston Jr emerged from the youth pathway. In the 1968 European Cup final, all four United goals against Benfica were scored by homegrown players: Bobby Charlton (two), Best and Brian Kidd.

1970s–1980s

The next generation

Arthur Albiston, Norman Whiteside, Mark Hughes, Clayton Blackmore and Sammy McIlroy carried the tradition through changing teams and managers.

1992 · Class of '92

Beckham and the golden generation

David Beckham, Ryan Giggs, Paul Scholes, Gary and Phil Neville, Nicky Butt and their teammates won the 1992 FA Youth Cup and grew into first-team regulars. Their contribution helped deliver the Treble in 1999 and years of domestic dominance.

2000s

Champions from the academy

Wes Brown, John O'Shea and Darren Fletcher followed the pathway into trophy-winning first teams. The academy's influence continued beyond the famous Class of '92.

2010s · Modern graduates

Rashford, McTominay and more

Marcus Rashford became a first-team regular after his breakthrough in 2016. Scott McTominay joined United's academy at age five, made his senior debut in 2017 and won the 2023 League Cup and 2024 FA Cup before leaving for Napoli in 2024. His story remains part of United's academy history.

2020s · New generation

Mainoo and the FA Youth Cup generation

Kobbie Mainoo progressed from the 2022 FA Youth Cup-winning side to the senior team, England and a goal in the 2024 FA Cup final. Alejandro Garnacho also emerged from that youth generation and scored in the same final. The pathway continues with Shea Lacey, Jack and Tyler Fletcher, Bendito Mantato and other young players.

A remarkable record: United's official history records a homegrown player in every first-team matchday squad since October 1937—more than 4,000 consecutive games by the club's November 2026 account. The academy has won 11 FA Youth Cups: 1953, 1954, 1955, 1956, 1957, 1964, 1992, 1995, 2003, 2011 and 2022.

The ownership question · 2003–2026

The Glazer takeover: the cost, the consequences and the questions.

This chapter sets out the documented history behind two decades of supporter opposition. It separates financial facts, sporting outcomes, criticism and the owners' own reported arguments.

2001–2004 · The Rock of Gibraltar dispute

Ferguson, Magnier and McManus fall out

Sir Alex Ferguson had a dispute with Irish businessmen John Magnier and J.P. McManus over the ownership and stud rights of champion racehorse Rock of Gibraltar. Ferguson believed he had a half-share that included breeding rights; Magnier's side disputed his claim. Ferguson began legal action in November 2003. The dispute was settled out of court in March 2004. Magnier and McManus were also major United shareholders through Cubic Expression, and their relationship with Ferguson and the club's board became increasingly strained. The dispute was a major factor in the breakdown of relationships at board level, but it was not, by itself, the legal or financial cause of the Glazer takeover.

12 May 2005 · The decisive share sale

Magnier and McManus sell their 28.7% stake to Glazer

Malcolm Glazer had already been buying United shares since 2003. The crucial turning point came when Magnier and McManus sold their 28.7% holding to Glazer's acquisition vehicle. That lifted Glazer's stake to 56.9%, giving him a controlling interest and triggering a bid for the remaining shares. The earlier Ferguson–Magnier dispute helps explain the fractured relationships around the club, while the sale of this large shareholding explains how Glazer secured control. The takeover was completed in stages during 2005.

2003–2004 · Building a stake

Malcolm Glazer begins buying shares

Malcolm Glazer began acquiring Manchester United shares in 2003 and steadily increased his holding. The move drew opposition from supporters concerned about a leveraged takeover and the possibility that debt would be placed on the club.

May–June 2005 · Takeover completed

A leveraged buyout changes the balance sheet

The Glazer family secured control in 2005 through a deal largely funded by borrowing. BBC Verify reports that United's gross debt rose from about £50m before the takeover to £604m in 2006. The club's board had warned that such borrowing was not prudent and risked a downward spiral in team and financial performance.

2005–2024 · The financial cost

Interest, dividends, fees and debt repayments

BBC Verify's analysis of published accounts estimates that around £1.187bn left the club between 2005 and 2024 through £815m in debt interest, £166m in dividends, £10m in management and administration fees to Glazer family companies, and £197m in external net debt repayments. This is a reported estimate of cash outflows associated with the ownership structure—not a claim that every pound would otherwise have been spent on transfers or facilities.

2010 · Supporter protests

Green and gold returns

Supporters revived the club's historic green-and-gold colours as a protest against the owners. The campaign became a visible expression of opposition to the debt-funded takeover and demands for change.

2012–2022 · Shares sold

The New York listing and share sales

Manchester United listed shares on the New York Stock Exchange in 2012. BBC Verify reports that between 2012 and 2022 the Glazer family sold shares worth £555m, with £484m going directly to family members and £71m used to partly repay acquisition debt.

2013 onwards · The post-Ferguson era

Sporting standards and leadership under scrutiny

Sir Alex Ferguson retired in 2013. United continued to win trophies under Glazer ownership, but the post-Ferguson period brought repeated managerial changes and a prolonged struggle to re-establish consistent title challenges. BBC Verify counted 15 major trophies during the family's ownership, five of them after Ferguson's retirement. The record is mixed and should be judged against the club's ambitions and resources.

2021 · European Super League

Supporter anger reaches a new peak

United's involvement in the proposed European Super League intensified opposition to the owners. Supporters protested, and the planned Premier League match against Liverpool at Old Trafford was postponed after a major demonstration at the stadium.

2022–2023 · Strategic review

Sale process announced, then a minority deal

In November 2022, the club announced it would explore strategic alternatives, including a possible sale. After a lengthy process, Sir Jim Ratcliffe's INEOS completed an investment for a minority stake in 2024 and took responsibility for football operations. The Glazer family remained significant shareholders and retained substantial voting influence.

December 2023–February 2024 · INEOS enters

Jim Ratcliffe buys a minority stake—not the club

After the Glazers announced a strategic review in November 2022, British billionaire Sir Jim Ratcliffe and INEOS agreed a deal in December 2023. It completed in February 2024. Ratcliffe acquired a minority stake, with the final holding commonly reported as approximately 27.7%. INEOS was delegated responsibility for men's and women's football operations and the academy, and committed $300m for infrastructure investment. The Glazer family retained majority ownership and control. Ratcliffe's arrival therefore changed the football leadership structure but did not amount to a full sale or the end of Glazer ownership.

2024–2026 · Debt and infrastructure

The financial and stadium questions remain

BBC Verify reported gross debt of £547m in 2024. In February 2026, BBC Sport reported total debt and other liabilities of about £1.29bn at the end of 2025, including more than £500m in additional liabilities, most of which were outstanding transfer payments. The club has also faced sustained criticism over ageing facilities and the future of Old Trafford. Ratcliffe's 2024 deal included a further £236m investment for infrastructure, according to BBC Verify.

September–November 2026 · Avram Glazer's reported remarks

“The value” of United is not dependent on results?

Reports published on 5 November 2026 say Avram Glazer told investors at Deutsche Bank's Sports Investment Conference in London in September that Manchester United's commercial value was not dependent on success on the pitch. He reportedly compared United's global brand with the New York Yankees and Dallas Cowboys. The remarks were reported by The Athletic and relayed by Sky Sports and other outlets; the public reports summarise his comments rather than providing a full transcript. Supporters may reasonably ask whether a club's commercial strength should ever be treated as separate from its footballing performance, heritage and match-going community.

What the evidence shows—and what it does not

The takeover's debt burden and subsequent financing costs are documented. So are the club's commercial growth and trophies during the same ownership period. It would be inaccurate to say the Glazers caused every sporting failure, every transfer decision or every infrastructure problem. Our campaign argues that ownership should be judged by transparent finances, long-term investment, football results, stadium stewardship and meaningful supporter representation.

The United sagas.

Football history is not only trophies. It is also arguments, turning points, personalities, near-misses, comebacks and decisions that changed the club. Each account explains what happened, why it mattered and what remains disputed.

1902 · Survival

The rescue and rebirth of Newton Heath

Financial trouble threatened Newton Heath. Investment associated with John Henry Davies helped secure the club's future, and the Manchester United name was adopted in 1902. This is the origin of the modern identity.

From financial danger to a rebuilt Old Trafford

United faced relegation and financial trouble in the 1930s. The Second World War damaged Old Trafford, forcing the club to play at Maine Road while its home was reconstructed. The post-war revival laid foundations for Busby's team.

The Munich Air Disaster and the promise interrupted

On 6 February 1958, the crash took 23 lives, including eight United players. The tragedy affected families, teammates, staff, journalists and the wider football community. The rebuilding that followed culminated in the 1968 European Cup. This story is told with dignity, not spectacle.

United went down in 1974, returned as Second Division champions in 1975 and won the FA Cup in 1977 against Liverpool. The sequence shows that the club's history includes difficult seasons and recovery, not only dominance.

The Selhurst Park incident and its aftermath

Eric Cantona was sent off at Crystal Palace in January 1995 and then attacked a spectator in the stands. He received a lengthy football ban and community service. The season that kept refusing to end

United won the Premier League, FA Cup and Champions League in 1998–99. The run included the FA Cup semi-final replay against Arsenal, a comeback against Juventus and two late goals against Bayern Munich. Keane's semi-final performance and suspension for the final are central parts of the story.

The founding principles

The United Supporters' Charter.

A proposed public charter for discussion. It describes standards this initiative believes should guide the club and the movement itself.

1. Supporters are stakeholders

Supporters sustain the club's culture across generations and should have meaningful, structured representation in major decisions.

2. Transparent finances

Publish accessible explanations of debt, cash flow, investment, dividends, executive remuneration and long-term liabilities.

3. Democratic voice

Build clear ways for eligible supporters to elect representatives, scrutinise decisions and challenge leadership peacefully.

4. Protect the football institution

Prioritise sustainable sporting planning, academy development, women's football, facilities and the club's long-term identity.

5. Respect and access

Recognise match-going, local, international, disabled, younger and future supporters; make consultation accessible and respectful.

6. Accountability applies to us too

Disclose our own governance, funding, conflicts, moderation rules and limitations. Never claim to represent every supporter.

Understanding the alternatives

What could supporter ownership look like?

Supporter ownership is not one single legal design. Models vary between member-owned clubs, supporter trusts holding shares, fan representation on boards and hybrid structures. Any proposal for United would need independent legal, financial and governance analysis.

1. Membership

Eligible supporters join under clear rules, with transparent fees or free access where possible and no extra votes for wealth.

2. Democratic elections

Members elect representatives for defined terms, with published candidates, voting rules and conflict declarations.

3. Board representation

Supporter representatives have defined rights, access to relevant information and duties to act in the club's long-term interests.

4. Financial safeguards

Independent audits, published accounts, borrowing limits and rules for related-party transactions protect the institution.

5. Heritage protections

Constitutional safeguards could cover club identity, stadium decisions, academy commitments and major structural changes.

6. A credible transition

Any move to ownership requires a viable purchase structure, funding, due diligence, regulatory approval and supporter consent.

Important distinction: supporter ownership is our long-term ambition, not an existing arrangement, guaranteed outcome or ready-made legal plan. We will explain different models fairly and publish their trade-offs.

Check the claim

United: myth, fact and context.

A learning page should help readers test claims, not just repeat them. These examples show how we will distinguish confirmed facts from shorthand and opinion.

Fact check

“United were relegated in 1978.”

Not correct. United were relegated at the end of 1973–74 and won promotion in 1974–75. They finished 10th in 1977–78.

Fact check

“The Glazers bought United in 2024.”

Incorrect. The Glazer takeover was completed in 2005. INEOS acquired a minority stake in 2024 while the Glazers retained majority ownership.

Context

“The 1990 FA Cup alone saved Ferguson.”

The cup win was an important turning point during a period of pressure, but the full story includes the club's longer-term rebuilding, recruitment and subsequent development.

Context

“The takeover explains every bad result.”

Ownership and financial decisions can be scrutinised, but individual sporting outcomes also involve managers, recruitment, players, injuries, opponents and many other factors.

Fact check

“United have won three European Cups.”

Correct for the men's first team: 1968, 1999 and 2008. The club's official honours page is the reference point for the current trophy count.

Context

“A supporter poll represents all United fans.”

Not necessarily. Every poll needs its question, dates, response count and method published. An open online poll is not a formal vote of the global fanbase.

Our standard: every new fact-check should link to primary records or reputable reporting, include a publication date and be updated when new evidence changes the picture.
A clear starting point

Supporter questions, answered.

Short answers to the questions a new visitor may have about the club and this initiative.

When was Manchester United founded?

The club traces its origins to 1878, when it was formed as Newton Heath LYR Football Club. It adopted the Manchester United name in 1902.

Why is Old Trafford important?

It has been United's home since 1910, apart from the wartime period when damage forced the club to play home matches at Maine Road during reconstruction.

What was the Munich Air Disaster?

The 1958 air crash killed 23 people, including eight United players. The club remembers all those who died and the people and families affected.

What is the Busby Babes legacy?

It represents Matt Busby's commitment to youth development and the team built around young players, whose story was profoundly changed by Munich.

What does this initiative want?

It advocates transparency, accountability, meaningful supporter representation and full supporter ownership as a long-term ambition. It is independent and is not the club's official voice.

Can I join or vote today?

Not yet. Polls and submissions remain marked as opening soon until a secure, moderated service and suitable adult oversight are in place. No personal contact details are needed to read or share the site.

The long view

A yearbook of Manchester United history.

A structured guide to the major eras, milestones and records. The club's own decade archive is the starting point for deeper research; this page will keep expanding season by season as facts are checked.

1878–1909

Railway origins, Newton Heath, survival in 1902, entry to the First Division, first league championship and first FA Cup.

1910–1919

Old Trafford opens, the 1911 league title, wartime football and the impact of the First World War.

1920–1929

League football resumes, difficult league campaigns, relegation in 1922 and promotion back in 1925.

1930–1939

Financial crisis, relegation and promotion battles, James W. Gibson's rescue and the development of youth football.

1940–1949

Wartime damage to Old Trafford, Maine Road ground-sharing, Busby's appointment, the 1948 FA Cup and the stadium reopening.

1950–1959

League titles, the Busby Babes, European campaigns and the Munich Air Disaster in 1958.

1960–1969

Rebuilding, the Holy Trinity, three Ballon d'Or winners, league titles and the 1968 European Cup.

1970–1979

Busby's departure, managerial changes, 1974 relegation, 1975 promotion and the 1977 FA Cup.

1980–1989

Ron Atkinson's cup-winning sides, league-title near misses and Ferguson's arrival in 1986.

1990–1999

The 1990 FA Cup, Cantona, the Premier League era, Class of '92 and the 1999 Treble.

2000–2009

Three consecutive league titles, the 2005 takeover, Ronaldo's rise, the 2008 Champions League and Club World Cup.

2010–2019

Ferguson's final title, the post-Ferguson transition, domestic cups, Europa League and women's team launch.

2020–2026

Super League backlash, academy graduates, ownership review, INEOS minority investment and the latest football era.

A statement of purpose

The United We Want.

Our vision for the club's next generation.

Manchester United began with workers, grew through communities, survived hardship, mourned its lost, rebuilt its teams and created memories that travelled far beyond Manchester.

We believe the club's future should be shaped with the people who give it meaning. Supporters should have a real voice, transparent information and a meaningful stake in decisions that shape their club.

We want responsible finances, a world-class academy, strong women's football, ambitious teams, facilities worthy of the club's history and a genuine connection with local and global communities.

We want leadership that explains its decisions, accepts scrutiny and protects the institution beyond any one owner, executive, manager or player.

We will pursue this through learning, evidence, respectful debate, lawful peaceful campaigning and democratic organisation. We will not harass, threaten, mislead or pretend to speak for every supporter.

Our club. Our history. Our voice.

More than results

What United must never lose.

The things that give the badge meaning are bigger than a league table.

Memory and respect

Remember those lost in Munich and both World Wars, and treat the club's history with dignity.

The academy pathway

Protect the tradition of developing young players and giving them a genuine route to the first team.

Footballing ambition

Compete with courage, intelligence and commitment while building teams that can last.

Supporter culture

Respect the Stretford End, match-going supporters, fan groups and people who carry United around the world.

Women's football

Recognise Manchester United Women as part of the club's present and future, with investment and visibility.

Community connection

Keep Manchester, its neighbourhoods and the wider football community central to the club's responsibilities.

01 / Why we exist

United belongs in the hands of its people.

Manchester United is more than a business. It is a football institution shaped by generations of supporters around the world. We believe those supporters deserve a meaningful voice in its future.

Our long-term ambition is full supporter ownership, supported by democratic governance, responsible finances and transparent decision-making. Achieving this would require serious legal, financial and organisational work.

Our founding belief
THE PEOPLE'S CLUB.
THE PEOPLE'S FUTURE.

Not a promise of an easy road. A commitment to organise, learn, listen and pursue meaningful change together.

02 / What we stand for

Five principles. One direction.

These are the campaign's founding demands and principles. They guide our public work and the standards we expect from our own leadership.

01

End Glazer family control

Campaign for an end to Glazer family control of Manchester United.

02

Full supporter ownership

Make 100% supporter ownership our long-term ambition, subject to a credible and viable route.

03

Democratic governance

Support meaningful representation, transparent elections and one-member-one-vote principles.

04

Financial transparency

Demand clear reporting, responsible stewardship and accountability for major decisions.

05

Protect United's heritage

Respect the club's history, identity, supporters and connection to its communities.

+

Peaceful, lawful action

Build change through education, dialogue and peaceful democratic campaigning.

03 / How we intend to operate

A movement accountable to its members.

We are developing a democratic structure in which members shape priorities, elect representatives and can hold leadership accountable.

Founding-stage proposal — not yet a registered organisation

One eligible member, one vote

No extra voting power for wealth, donations, status or popularity.

Nine-member elected council

A proposed council with defined roles, fixed terms and term limits.

Open accountability

Published decisions, regular reports, conflict declarations and financial oversight.

The governance framework is a proposal for discussion. Formal membership, elections, fundraising and legal registration will only proceed after appropriate adult oversight, safeguarding and independent legal advice are in place.
